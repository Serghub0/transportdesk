What is this tool for?
When you need to connect two sites over fiber, this tool tells you exactly which optical transceiver (SFP, SFP+, QSFP28, QSFP-DD, etc.) to order, and whether your cable plant has enough optical budget to support the link at that speed and distance. It calculates all the loss components and gives a clear Pass or Fail verdict.

Choose your data rate (1G through 800G), your target distance from the dropdown — shown in both km and miles — and whether you are running multimode (MMF, data center) or single-mode fiber (SMF, anything over 500 m).
Real example
You are connecting a new router at Cypress Hub to a customer handoff at Tampa POP, 40 km apart, running 100G over SMF OS2. Select 100G · 40 km (24.9 mi) · SMF OS2.

1
What is a loopback test?
A loopback test verifies that both fibers in a pair are intact and healthy end-to-end. You physically connect the TX port to the RX port at the far-end cabinet using a loopback plug. Your OTDR or power meter at the near end then measures the signal traveling down fiber A, through the plug, and back up fiber B. This confirms both strands are working in a single test.
A-side TX
── fiber A ──
Z-side loopback plug
── fiber B ──
A-side RX
Round-trip loss = (one-way loss × 2) + plug insertion loss
2
Name A-side and Z-side cabinets — use real cabinet IDs
Enter the full cabinet name and location for each end. This appears in the verdict so you have a permanent record of exactly which cabinet pair was tested. Use the same naming convention as your work order or splice records.
Real example
A-Side / Cabinet: MDF Cabinet — Cypress, FL
Z-Side / Cabinet: MMP Cabinet — Tampa, FL

These are the two ends of the dark fiber span you are accepting. The A-side is always where your test equipment sits. The Z-side is the far end where your tech plugs in the loopback adapter.
3
Enter the distance — all three units sync automatically
Type the span length in km, miles, or kft — the other two fields fill in automatically. The distance is critical: it is used to calculate the expected loss per kft and flag if the one-way loss is abnormally high for that length of fiber.
Real example
Your OTDR trace shows the fiber is 13.486 kft. Type that in the kft box → auto-fills 4.110 km and 2.554 mi. Now the tool knows that at 0.13 dB/kft the maximum acceptable one-way loss is 1.753 dB. Anything higher is flagged as a problem.
4
Enter one-way OTDR loss and measured round-trip
One-way loss comes from your OTDR printout for that fiber. Measured round-trip comes from your optical power meter reading at the A-side after the loopback plug is installed at the Z-side. The tool then compares the measured value against the expected round-trip budget.

✗ 500 dB on 13.486 kft = 37 dB/kft — far exceeds 0.13 limit → FAIL

What is this tool for?
When you need to splice a new network node into an existing live fiber span — a new ROADM, a new splice case, a new POP cabinet — this tool tells you whether the optical budget will still hold after the cut. A node insertion splits one span into two shorter sub-spans and adds the node's own optical loss. If either sub-span then exceeds the transceiver budget, the link will fail when you restore traffic. 1
What is "Budget Data"?
Every optical transceiver has a fixed optical power budget — the maximum total loss (in dB) it can handle between the transmitter and the receiver before the signal becomes too weak to decode. Think of it like a spending budget: the fiber distance, connectors, splices, and a safety margin all spend from that budget. If the total spent exceeds the budget, the link fails. "Budget Data" in the tool header means this reference contains all the optical budget figures you need to design and verify fiber links.
Budget examples by transceiver class
LR (Long Reach): 12 dB — for links up to 10 km on SMF
ER (Extended Reach): 17 dB — for links up to 40 km on SMF
ZR (Ultra-Long): 25 dB — for links up to 80 km on SMF
ZR+ (Coherent): managed per EDFA span — 500 km to 2,000+ km
2

All conditions are met. The total required budget (fiber + connectors + splices + margin) is less than what the transceiver provides, and the loss/kft is within the acceptable range for the distance. The link is safe to commission.

6
Assessment badges (OTDR and Loopback tabs)
Each fiber strand gets a badge based on its average loss per kft, which is the standard field acceptance measurement for dark fiber. These thresholds match TIA-568 and common carrier acceptance standards.
Cleanest trace — ≤ 0.08 dB/kft Best — low loss — ≤ 0.10 dB/kft Fair / acceptable — ≤ 0.11 dB/kft Acceptable — elevated — ≤ 0.13 dB/kft Needs review — > 0.13 dB/kft
1
What numbers to pull from an OSP OTDR printout
When you receive an OTDR report from your OSP (outside plant) contractor, look for two numbers per fiber in the summary section — not the event table. You want the total end-to-end loss in dB and the total fiber length in kft or km. These are typically on the first or last page of the report labeled "Total Loss" and "Fiber Length" or "Cable Length."
Real example — from a Viavi/EXFO printout
Fiber 001: Length = 13.486 kft  ·  Total Loss = 1.575 dB
Fiber 002: Length = 13.486 kft  ·  Total Loss = 1.798 dB
Enter exactly those numbers into the OTDR Tester. The tool calculates avg loss/kft and tells you if each fiber meets acceptance.
2
One-way vs. bi-directional OTDR testing
A one-way OTDR test measures from one end only. A bi-directional test averages the results from both ends — this is the OSP industry standard for final acceptance because it eliminates directional anomalies like splice asymmetry. If your OSP provides bi-directional results, use the averaged total loss value in the OTDR Tester.
Which to use
Acceptance testing: Use bi-directional averaged result if available.
Troubleshooting: Use one-way — it shows where along the fiber the problem is.
3
Fusion vs. Mechanical splices — the loss difference matters
This is one of the most important things to verify with your OSP contractor before accepting a fiber plant.
Splice type comparison
Fusion splice: 0.01–0.05 dB each · Industry standard for all permanent OSP dark fiber · The splicer melts the two fiber ends together — nearly lossless · This is what HWC should require on all permanent installs

Mechanical splice: 0.70–1.50 dB each · 15× more loss than fusion · Used for emergency field restoration only when a fusion splicer is not available · Never acceptable on a permanent fiber plant

Impact example: A span with 8 splices:
· Fusion: 8 × 0.05 = 0.40 dB total splice loss
· Mechanical: 8 × 1.00 = 8.00 dB total splice loss — could fail the entire link budget
4
Safety margin — overall, not per splice
The 3 dB safety margin in the Link Analyzer is applied once to the entire link — not per splice. It covers aging of connectors and splices over time, temperature variation, future repair splices, and test equipment tolerance. The splice loss per-unit is already calculated separately based on your splice count and type.
How the budget breaks down
Fiber loss: 3.50 dB  +  Connectors (4 × 0.25): 1.00 dB  +  Splices (4 × 0.05): 0.20 dB  +  Overall margin: 3.00 dB = 7.70 dB total required
Transceiver budget (10G LR): 12 dB → PASS with 4.30 dB headroom
5
What to do when a fiber fails acceptance
If a fiber shows "Needs review" (loss/kft > 0.13 dB/kft), escalate in this order:
Investigation steps
Step 1: Re-run the OTDR from both ends — confirm the loss is real, not a test setup error.
Step 2: Look at the OTDR event table — find which event (splice, connector, bend) is causing the excess loss.
Step 3: If a splice — request the OSP contractor re-splice that specific location.
Step 4: If a connector — clean and re-test. A dirty connector can add 0.5–2 dB alone.
Step 5: If the loss is distributed evenly — may indicate a microbend issue from improper installation. Requires route investigation.
6
Verifying span distance matches your work order
The fiber length on the OTDR should be within 2–3% of the route distance on your work order. A big discrepancy means either the route took a longer path than planned (common in conduit systems with curves) or the OTDR refractive index setting is wrong. Use the Miles ↔ KM converter in the OTDR tab to verify your work order distance against the OTDR reading.
